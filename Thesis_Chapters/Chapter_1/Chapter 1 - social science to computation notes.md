# Chapter 1: movement from social science to computation

Reviewed source: `Thesis Chapter 1.pdf` (12 pages). All page numbers below refer to PDF page order.

The chapter moves gradually toward computation. Sections 1.1-1.3 establish the social science framework; section 1.4 prepares the relational research problem; section 1.5 explicitly introduces computational analysis. The earliest clear methodological rationale is the closing paragraph of section 1.4 on page 9. The explicit computational turn is the sentence beginning “Computational analysis supplements…” on page 10.

In the marked PDF, yellow highlights identify preparatory bridges; green identifies the explicit computational shift. The numbered highlights contain explanatory PDF comments. The chapter wording is preserved, and the original PDF is unchanged.

## 01. PDF page 8: Conceptual preparation

> As the project's central research framework argues, salt routes functioned as the infrastructural backbone of wider multi-commodity networks that connected the Tibetan Plateau with Ladakh, Kashmir, Baltistan, Himachal, and adjoining Himalayan regions.

The discussion moves from the historical circulation of salt to your own framework of connected routes, regions, and commodities. This prepares a relational perspective that could support computational modelling. It remains a historical claim here: the word networks does not itself establish a computational method.

## 02. PDF page 9: Research gap that motivates the method

> While these studies have substantially enriched understanding of Himalayan exchange, the relationships between commodities, institutions, routes, and political actors often remain dispersed across travel narratives, gazetteers, administrative reports, treaties, and ethnographic observations. It is therefore challenging to examine the Himalayan salt economy as a unified system. This limitation is particularly evident when attempting to reconstruct the wider networks through which salt linked pastoral communities, monasteries, state authorities, frontier markets, and other commodities.

This is the strongest preparatory bridge in section 1.4. You identify a problem of assembling relationships scattered across sources and examining them together. That supplies a rationale for computational network analysis, although no computational procedure is introduced in this passage.

## 03. PDF page 10: From historiographical gap to research approach

> The principal gap, therefore, concerns the reconstruction of connections across commodities, routes, institutions, and political authorities. This study is situated within this historiographical context. Instead of supplanting this historical and anthropological research, this thesis aims to supplement these works by bringing together archival evidence on the Western Himalayan salt trade. In doing so, it uses an interdisciplinary methodology based on historical analysis of archival evidence to explore the interconnections between commodities, routes, institutions and political agents. The objective is not simply to reconstruct individual episodes of trade but to understand the wider networks through which salt shaped frontier economies and political authority during the colonial period.

Here the literature review becomes a statement of your own research approach: assembling archival evidence to reconstruct connections. This is the immediate bridge into computation. Interdisciplinary methodology still leaves the computational procedure unspecified; the next sentence supplies the explicit signpost.

## 04. PDF page 10: Explicit shift to computational analysis

> Computational analysis supplements conventional historical analysis by exploring the intricate network of transactions recorded in dispersed archives (Painter et al., 2019).

This is the clearest and first explicit move into computational analysis in the chapter body. You name computation and give it a supporting role alongside historical interpretation. The method itself is only signposted: this chapter does not explain how archival evidence becomes a network representation or how that representation will be analysed.

## 05. PDF page 11: Archives as the basis for network construction

> The archives serve as the empirical base for building historical networks of connections established via the salt trade.

This carries the transition forward by connecting the source corpus to the construction of historical networks. It is an empirical bridge toward a possible computational representation, rather than a technical explanation. The preceding sentences introduce the next chapter as a discussion of sources, so this is not yet a full move into computational methods.

## How to read these marks

“Network” also describes historical and social relationships. Its use in the introduction, in the discussion of netsang, or in the account of monasteries does not automatically signal computation. Mark 01 is included only as an early conceptual preparation, not as a computational passage.

The progression is: socially embedded exchange → dispersed evidence about relationships → reconstruction of those relationships → computational analysis → archival basis for network construction. Social science remains the interpretive framework throughout.

This is a transition review only. It does not rewrite the chapter or verify the cited scholarship. The locations of the preparatory bridges are interpretive; only mark 04 explicitly names computation.
