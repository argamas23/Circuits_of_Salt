# Nepal–Tibet salt scholarship: evidence and comparisons for Chapter 1

Research memorandum, 27 October 2026. This supports Samagra Bharti's *Circuits of Salt*, whose principal case remains Ladakh/Rupshu–Tso Kar. Nepal, Dolpo, Humla, Khumbu and eastern Tibet are comparative cases, not substitutes for evidence about Ladakh. No chapter or master-bibliography text has been changed.

## Main finding

The existing local collection already contains a much stronger anthropology and political economy of salt than Chapter 1 currently deploys. In particular, Fürer-Haimendorf, Ross and Lecoq should be discussed as authors with different explanations, rather than mined for examples to illustrate Mauss or Polanyi. Together they make it difficult to sustain an opposition between an earlier altruistic barter economy and a later profit-seeking market economy. Their cases contain profit, fluctuating exchange rates, lending, monopoly, paid labour, religious giving, household subsistence and durable friendship at the same time. What changes is their institutional combination and the distribution of benefits.

**Most useful immediate addition:** a sustained comparison of Fürer-Haimendorf's Thak Khola and Tarali cases with Ross's Humla findings, followed by Lecoq on the practical and ritual organisation of caravans. Lazcano supplies a larger Tibetan comparison and further source leads. Fisher and Bauer are essential additions, but the level of access below must be preserved when citing them.

## 1. Fürer-Haimendorf: commerce, social relations and political authority

**Reference:** Fürer-Haimendorf, C. von (1975) *Himalayan Traders: Life in Highland Nepal*. London: John Murray. The actual title is *Himalayan Traders*, not *Traders of Highland Nepal*. Local scan: `Books/Himalayan-Traders-Life-In-Highland-Nepal.pdf`; OCR: `corpus/Himalayan-Traders-Life-In-Highland-Nepal.txt`. [Yale eHRAF record](https://ehrafworldcultures.yale.edu/cultures/ak06/documents/002); [Open Library edition records](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL5183005M/Himalayan_traders).

### Ecology does not itself explain commercial power

Printed pp. 181–187 (PDF pp. 209–215) explain why Thak Khola/Tukche became an entrepôt. Its position allowed highland pack animals and lowland travellers to meet without enduring the most difficult climate of the other region. Opposing seasonal constraints also created a need for storage: summer travel from Tibet was relatively easy when southern monsoon routes were difficult; southern circulation revived when the northern winter constrained travel. Traders could therefore make money by coordinating transport and storage across different seasons. The explanation is more precise than “ecological complementarity”: mediation across different calendars, animal capacities and transport sections created an economic role.

The same pages distinguish wealthy merchants who stayed at Tukche from people of Lo and Baragaon who increasingly undertook difficult northern journeys. Printed pp. 186–187 separate salt/grain barter from wool and live-animal trade whose prices were generally expressed in money. The author notes that middlemen attained wealth unavailable to the producers with whom they traded. **Thesis implication:** production, carrying and brokering should be distinguished. Shared dependence on an exchange system did not entail equal returns.

### Salt monopoly combined fiscal and judicial power

Printed pp. 142–147 (PDF pp. 166–171) reconstruct customs farming and the subba families. On pp. 142–143 the government order of 1862 introduced duties and a salt monopoly; the contract was auctioned, the contractor received the title *subba*, and in this remote area he also exercised magistrate's powers. Printed pp. 145–146 describe compulsory purchase of incoming salt and sale to southern customers, rivalry over contracts, and abolition of the monopoly in 1928. Political authority and commodity control were entwined long before the post-1959 border regime.

Printed pp. 188–189 (PDF pp. 216–217) give the mechanisms: designated routes and selling points, constraints on the distance different trading populations could carry salt, required dealings with the contractor, duties on grain, and fines for using prohibited routes. **Thesis implication:** a claim that “the state later intruded upon previously autonomous exchange” is inadequate unless period and corridor are specified. States could also create local mercantile power and channel commercial routes through selected intermediaries.

**Chronological warning:** Haimendorf is not internally consistent. Printed pp. 142–143 give an 1862 order, whereas p. 188 refers to abolition of the preceding arrangement in 1860 and a sanad in 1861; p. 188 gives 1860–1932 for the monopoly, although pp. 146 and 189 explicitly date its abolition to 1928. Lecoq p. 44 gives 1862–1928. Do not present a seamless precise chronology assembled from these passages. Use “nineteenth-century customs farming” for Chapter 1, and verify the dated original orders for Chapter 2.

### Credit and accumulation were organised socially

Printed pp. 197–201 (PDF pp. 227–231) discuss *dhigur*, a rotating credit association, as a way for traders to raise cash without regular banks. The arrangements include guarantors, lotteries or bidding for the pooled sum, treasurers, interest and transferable shares. Printed p. 199 records a temporary 1959 ban on forming new associations because the resulting claims and obligations had become too complex. The author also limits the institution geographically: it was familiar among Thakalis, some Baragaon traders and other groups, but apparently not practised in Dolpo and Tichurong at the time.

Printed pp. 199–201 follow Tsiring Tensing's business history. Loans helped him enter salt/grain exchange; he extended into Indian manufactured and religious goods, suffered animal losses when recovering debts, and used a dhigur and renewed borrowing to recover. This is a useful example of commercial mobility, debt, risk and religious actors participating in finance. It does not establish that every salt trader used the same institutions. **Thesis implication:** an embedded transaction can involve interest and calculated profit. “Embeddedness” needs an identified institutional mechanism, not a presumed absence of commercial calculation.

### Partnership included housing, care and paid work

Printed pp. 205–211 (PDF pp. 235–241) describe Dolpo–Tichurong relations. During the winter, Dolpo people could occupy houses vacated by Tarali traders, care for animals and relatives, clear snow and undertake farm and building tasks. Printed p. 206 presents this as mutual dependence while also recording paid labour, including grain wages for men and women and different rates for different tasks. Printed p. 211 describes short-term storage through ceremonial friends, changing travel partners, and trading parties containing both men and women. The institutional package therefore included hospitality, household reproduction, work and commercial dealings, rather than a single undifferentiated form of reciprocity.

The author is explicit about uncertainty on printed p. 209: he records rules restricting Taralis' northern movement and Dolpo people's southern movement, but says the authority behind these rules is unclear. This is a useful model of honest historical qualification.

### Gifts and commerce: a direct challenge to an easy Maussian reading

Printed pp. 294–298 (PDF pp. 330–334) deserve explicit discussion in Chapter 1. On p. 294 caravans assembled for safety do not automatically own goods collectively: individuals retain goods and negotiate their deals. On p. 295 the author distinguishes commercial debts and gain-seeking transactions from wedding gifts and donations. He then identifies ceremonial friendship as an institution intertwining economic and social relationships. Printed pp. 296–297 describe *mit* in Nepali and *thouwu* among Sherpas: a formally established tie with ritual, hospitality and assistance in selling or storing goods, which can cross ethnic boundaries. Printed p. 298 considers how cash and open weekly markets might reduce the need for such close personal contacts.

This should be treated as an argument to evaluate. One can ask whether Haimendorf draws the distinction between business and gift too sharply; one should not cite him as if he simply asserted that every trade transaction was gift exchange. Nor should *mit*, *thouwu* and Dolpo *netsang* be presented as interchangeable institutions with identical rules.

### Gender, caste and identity are differentiated

Printed p. 288 (PDF p. 322) describes independent Sherpa women's trading and Thakali women's seasonal taverns, and emphasises women's decisions during men's absence. Printed pp. 281–282 (PDF pp. 315–316) show Hindu traders negotiating dietary and ritual restrictions and building friendships with distant trading partners while having fewer ties with nearby communities whose economies competed rather than complemented one another. Printed pp. 299–301 (PDF pp. 335–337) connect salt-contracting, contact with Kathmandu officials, caste status and Thakali reform. Trade can preserve boundaries and encourage selective cultural change; commercial contact does not guarantee cultural fusion.

**Critical limitation:** the book sometimes assigns broad commercial capacities to “Buddhist” versus “Hindu” societies and ranks populations in a dated civilisational vocabulary. Its own Hindu trading cases complicate these generalisations. Use its specific observations and institutional histories without adopting an essentialist explanation of success. Later Thakali scholarship, especially Vinding and Manzardo, is needed to test its account of earlier Buddhist identity and subsequent Hinduisation.

## 2. Ross: adaptation, profit and labour in Humla

**Reference:** Ross, J. L. (1982–1983) ‘Adaptation to a changing salt trade: The view from Humla’, *Contributions to Nepalese Studies*, 10(1–2), pp. 43–49. The masthead reads December 1982/June 1983. Local: `Books/HUMLA_NEPAL.pdf`; OCR: `corpus/HUMLA_NEPAL.txt`. [ICIMOD catalogue](https://lib.icimod.org/records/sp7g3-mtw19); [article endpoint](https://lib.icimod.org/records/sp7g3-mtw19/files/7002.pdf?download=1). Some later indexes report pp. 43–50; the local article ends on printed p. 49.

Printed p. 43 identifies both Tibetan-speaking Buddhist and high-caste Thakuri/Chhetri Hindu traders and presents favourable salt/grain exchange differentials as a source of substantial profit. It also records an option used particularly by Limi inhabitants: collecting salt at its source after paying Tibetan officials, rather than always buying through the same chain of intermediaries. This requires qualifying broad descriptions of access as exclusive everywhere.

Printed p. 44 states that exchange rates varied by individual, season, supply and demand, while ritualised friendship with Purang partners helped secure dependable supplies and minimum grain rates. Barter, profit calculation and social ties coexisted. Trips also provided pasture; a salt journey cannot be understood solely as transporting one commodity as efficiently as possible.

Printed pp. 45–46 describe reorganisation after the consolidation of Chinese authority around Purang. Nepali traders increasingly had to use official depots, state-set exchange rates and measurement by weight rather than traditional volume. The important intervention was not merely drawing a line on a map: it changed who could trade, with whom, under which measure and at what rate. Meanwhile, southern routes and cheaper Indian salt altered the other end of the circuit.

Printed pp. 46–48 document adaptation in 1976–1977. Traders acquired Indian salt for cash, sometimes generated by selling an animal, and used both Indian and Tibetan salt in southern exchange. Printed p. 48 records 2,100 loads of Tibetan and 1,850 loads of Indian salt across the two studied villages. Thus Indian supplies had not simply displaced all salt caravans. Commodity provenance and trading circuits could change while mobile exchange persisted.

Printed p. 47 reports paid seasonal work for adults from twelve households, compensated through food, clothes and cash-equivalent payment, sometimes taken in animals. Such work could help a household acquire its own transport animals and trade independently. This supplies a concrete labour history that a literature review focused only on reciprocity would miss. Access to livestock, household labour and trading capital shaped participation.

Printed pp. 47–48 report different levels of participation and household grain contributions among the studied Thakuri and Tibetan-speaking populations. The findings should remain bounded to these communities and the 1976–1977 season. Note 1 on p. 48 dates fieldwork October 1975–July 1977. Ross's dramatic historical profit example on p. 45 is explicitly hypothetical; its percentage is not a measured regional rate of return and does not value all labour, animal and risk costs. Prefer the observed adaptation evidence over repeating that headline number.

**Contrast with Haimendorf:** Haimendorf printed pp. 202–203 describes Tukche's decline and mercantile emigration; Ross finds continued viable trade through combining salt sources in Humla. The cases support uneven regional reorganisation, not one Himalayan date of “collapse”. Their different places, periods and research scales matter.

## 3. Lecoq: the caravan as material and ceremonial work

**Reference:** Lecoq, P. (2022) ‘Salt routes and barter caravans in the Himalayan regions of Nepal and Tibet from an ethnographical perspective’, in Clarkson, P. B. and Santoro, C. M. (eds) *Caravans in Socio-Cultural Perspective: Past and Present*. Abingdon: Routledge, pp. 39–53. [DOI and publisher source](https://doi.org/10.4324/9781003179276-4). Local: `Books/Salt_routes_and_barter_caravans_in_the_H.pdf`. The author is Patrice Lecoq, not Santiago Lazcano; these are separate works.

Printed pp. 41–45 place Dolpo/Tarap and Mustang within multi-stage exchange, differentiate exchange venues, commodities and animals, and discuss state monopolies and Indian salt. Printed pp. 45–48 provide the most useful detail for an anthropology section: salt is repacked into bags suited to goats; caravans require equipment, food, loading and animal management; offerings precede departure and mark travel and return; established partners supply accommodation, food and enclosures; salt/grain exchanges coexist with wool, butter and manufactured goods. Ritual does not float above the economy: the account connects it to the uncertainties and relationships of travel.

Printed pp. 47–48 describe inherited relationships with ceremonial friends across ethnic and religious boundaries. The author states that negotiated barter values remain approximately steady across years and are not linked to market prices. **Compare explicitly with Ross p. 44 and Haimendorf pp. 184, 190:** both describe fluctuating rates. The discrepancy should become a research question about partner-specific terms, places, dates and evidence. Do not select the stable-ratio description as a universal Himalayan rule.

Printed pp. 48–50 discuss return rituals, caravan knowledge, pathways and later tourism or alternative commodities. The acknowledgements on p. 50 identify interviews in Mustang in 1985. This makes the chapter useful, but its 2022 publication date should not be mistaken for the date of all its ethnographic observations.

**Critical use:** the chapter's phrasing about an ancient way of life approaching disappearance risks flattening political and commercial diversity. It also draws on documentaries and travel publications alongside ethnography. Anchor claims in a named place and observation period and use Ross's adaptation findings as a corrective. The text does not provide adequate regional quantitative totals: p. 43 itself notes missing data before estimating caravan animals; avoid converting that estimate into an established network-wide scale.

## 4. Lazcano: Tibetan regional contrasts, gender and coercion

**Reference:** Lazcano, S. (2022) ‘The salt trips in Tibet and the Himalayas: Extraction and trade in pre-modern times’, *Revue d’Études Tibétaines*, 65, pp. 70–103. Local: `Books/Santiago_Lazcano.pdf`. [Original full issue](https://himalaya.socanth.cam.ac.uk/collections/journals/ret/pdf/ret_65.pdf).

Printed pp. 71–76 distinguish the Changthang lakes, Amdo/Tsaidam sources and Tsakalho saltpans and place salt in routes involving Tibetan administrations, Himalayan brokers and markets. Printed pp. 73–75 discuss taxes and intermediaries; p. 76 includes compulsory transport as part of fiscal burdens. This broadens the review beyond voluntary partnership without assuming identical extraction or governance in all three regions.

The clearest internal contrast is gender. Printed pp. 71–73 describe male salt-lake expeditions and their ritual restrictions; printed pp. 83–84 describe female labour in Tsakalho's wells and evaporation terraces. Therefore neither “salt was men's work” nor “women were confined to the household” is an adequate Tibetan/Himalayan generalisation. Distinguish extraction from caravan transport and retail, and identify which salt source is being discussed. The article is a synthesis with sources of uneven quality; precise claims of exclusively male or female labour should be checked against the relevant original ethnography rather than extended to new cases.

Printed pp. 90–91 report unequal and coercive exchange in the Drung/Nu and extended eastern Himalayan borderlands, linking salt dependence to high-interest debt and domination by chiefs, monasteries or intermediaries. **Comparison, not transfer:** this supplies a counter-case to equal ceremonial partnership; it does not show that Ladakhi netsang or Tso Kar extraction operated through the same relations. The underlying scholarly lead is Stéphane Gros's 2016 study of debt and imposed sovereignty.

Printed pp. 92–95 discuss collectivisation, border controls, later reopening, industrial production, altered commodities and heritage/tourism. The text itself qualifies its broad border-closure claim in p. 92 n. 63 by identifying exceptions in Limi/Upper Humla and renewed opportunities in Walung. Use these qualifications, not a single “1959 closed all Himalayan trade” sentence. Likewise its reference to Rupshu changing salt sources on p. 93 n. 66 must be compared with Ahmed's actual chronology before reuse.

**Source criticism:** do not import the introduction's broad claims about iodine, salt deficiency, or a simple marine origin of all Tibetan salt lakes. They require specialist verification. Statements about contemporary railways or the imminent extinction of caravans are time-bound and should not become general historical conclusions. The bibliography also contains errors: Bauer's *Dolpo Revisited* is listed with “2017” but identifies the 2014 journal issue; the author's university CV and journal contents establish 2014.

## 7. Comparison structure for a stronger literature review

| Problem | Agreement/commonality | Difference or argument requiring evaluation | Safe use in a Ladakh-centred thesis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ecology and circulation | Salt/grain exchange combines different environments and seasonal calendars | Tukche merchants could store and broker; Tarali and Humla households travelled; production and transport were not identical occupations | Ask which combination existed in Rupshu; do not infer institutional identity from similar goods |
| Reciprocity and profit | Personal ties lowered uncertainty and supported hospitality | Haimendorf distinguishes commercial gain from gifts; Ross's rates fluctuate; Lecoq describes comparatively stable partner terms | Review the tension openly; treat reciprocity as a set of practices, not proof of altruism |
| Authority | Routes and supplies were politically organised | Nepalese customs farmers, Tibetan taxes, local arbitrators and later official depots were different arrangements | Compare rights and mechanisms across dates and jurisdictions |
| Social inequality | Households needed access to animals, work and trading contacts | Salt contractors accumulated concentrated wealth; other traders worked on smaller scales; wage labour also existed | Track producers, carriers, brokers and revenue collectors separately |
| Gender | Household labour sustained movement and exchange | Tibetan male lake expeditions, female Tsakalho production, mixed Tarali parties and female Sherpa/Thakali enterprise | Specify site and task; do not extrapolate one gender division across the Himalayas |
| Change | Roads, border administration and competing supplies changed exchange | Tukche decline, Humla's combined Indian/Tibetan salt trade, and later shifts to other businesses were uneven | Distinguish colonial intervention, 1959/1962 changes and later infrastructure |
| Identity | Trade required crossing linguistic and social boundaries | Commercial interdependence could coexist with maintained boundaries or selective status reform | Avoid assuming every economic connection produced a homogeneous regional culture |

### Suggested claim–evidence sequence

1. Establish what Himalayan salt scholarship has already explained: ecological exchange, transport and seasonality, with Haimendorf's regional cases and Lecoq's practical account.
2. Introduce the debate about social relations: friendship, hospitality, household reproduction and labour alongside exchange rates, cash, credit and profit. Use Ross and Haimendorf to qualify a direct application of gift theory.
3. Compare political organisation: contractors and route restrictions existed before late twentieth-century border controls; later administrations changed terms, partners, units of measurement and permitted movement.
4. Add differentiated social experience: wealth, ownership of transport animals, wages, gendered tasks and identity. These are researchable dimensions of the salt economy, not detachable “cultural” additions.
5. End with uneven transformation and the remaining question for western Himalayan evidence: how did the specific production rights, intermediary relations and changing circuits of Rupshu–Tso Kar compare with these better-studied systems?

**Possible thesis contribution, stated as a question:** can a historically validated, multi-commodity representation clarify how salt circulation intersected wool, grain, livestock and authority across otherwise scattered western Himalayan records? The literature already knows that salt connected commodities and societies. Network visualisation does not establish a novel historical finding merely by illustrating that proposition. The contribution must lie in the evidence assembled, the particular spatial/temporal comparison or a measured result supported by source criticism.

## Remaining limits

This is a substantial targeted search and close reading, not proof that every relevant Himalayan publication has been located. Fisher/Bauer full-book page verification, the original Jest and Gros articles, and later Thakali scholarship remain important access gaps. General comparisons above are analytic inferences from the explicitly identified cases; they are not additional primary evidence about Ladakh.
