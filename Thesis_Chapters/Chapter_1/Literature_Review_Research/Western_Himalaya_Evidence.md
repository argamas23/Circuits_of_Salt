# Western Himalayan salt scholarship: evidence and historiographical comparisons

Prepared 27 October 2026. Research memo for expanding Chapter 1; the existing cited chapter and master bibliography have not been changed. Supervisor approval is not implied.

## Findings that should shape the revision

The scholarship already permits a substantially stronger argument than a general statement that salt trade was socially embedded. Rizvi reconstructs overlapping provisioning and commercial circuits; Ahmed examines the institutions of extraction and unequal rights; Alam explains the political construction of markets and borders; Dollfus and Singh et al. analyse later changes in pastoral production, mobility and social reproduction. They are complementary approaches with different scales, evidence and chronologies. They should be compared directly, not assembled as witnesses to a single timeless Himalayan system.

Three particularly useful tensions emerged from direct reading:

1. **The history of Tso Kar extraction is disputed.** Ahmed (1999, pp. 38–40) explicitly declines to resolve conflicting nineteenth-/twentieth-century written accounts and retrospective oral accounts of extraction before the 1960s. Singh et al. (2013, p. 9) nevertheless describe pre-1962 Tso Kar salt collection as established. This is a difference in evidential treatment, not a reason to select whichever account supports the thesis.
2. **The conversion of Mandi salt transactions to money was not necessarily comprehensive.** Alam (2007, pp. 175–178) explains the 1871 abolition of salt-for-grain/ghee and purchaser labour and calls the cash transition successful. The 1920 Mandi gazetteer still describes unpaid work by headload purchasers alongside cash charges on traders (printed pp. 163–164; PDF pp. 176–177). These passages require investigation of differentiated purchasers and possible later change. They rule out an unqualified claim that every non-monetary obligation disappeared.
3. **Connection is already a scholarly argument.** Alam (2021, pp. 173–174, 188–189) explicitly theorises commodity circuits as constitutive of borders. Rizvi (1996, pp. 97–103) already reconstructs connections across products, communities and markets. A defensible contribution is a source-attributed comparison of selected relations and salt's position within a defined corpus, not discovery of Himalayan interdependence or political commodity networks.

## Ahmed: extraction as organised work, unequal entitlement and identity

**Setting and method.** The chapter combines the author's 1992 residence in Rupshu and observations of that year's October extraction with interviews in Rupshu and Leh in 1992–1995, earlier historical writings, administrative reports and local narratives. It is especially valuable because it compares these kinds of evidence rather than treating oral tradition as a transparent chronological record. It does not provide a representative survey of all Himalayan salt producers.

**What this adds to the literature review.** Ahmed is the strongest local source for showing how an essential commodity was produced through institutions that were simultaneously cooperative and exclusionary. An account that stresses trust alone misses labour discipline, allocation, enforcement, legal claims, gender, taste and membership. Equally, the chapter does not justify claiming that every social institution was reducible to salt production.

**Source dependence.** Ahmed thanks Rizvi for access to a forthcoming trade study and cites it as “in press” (pp. 33, 47–48). Dollfus's Tso Kar discussion later explicitly quotes Ahmed. Agreement across those texts is not three wholly independent observations of earlier practice.

## Rizvi: provisioning, profits and the scale of comparison

**Method and contribution of the directly inspected book passages.** *Ladakh* is a regional historical synthesis. Its trade section distinguishes salt–grain–wool exchange from luxury and long-distance trade, but repeatedly reconnects these strands through actors, household organisation and markets. Unlike Ahmed's close examination of one extraction event, it follows movement over a much larger area. Its confident syntheses and approximate quantities need checking against the larger trade monograph and underlying records before numerical use.

- **Printed pp. 97–98:** salt, grain and wool are staples of a barter-based provisioning trade between complementary ecological areas. Ladakh's pashm trade brings cash and high-value production into that same setting. Ecological complementarity explains the incentive for exchange; it does not settle who captured its proceeds.
- **Printed p. 99:** Shamma participation in pashm developed alongside entrenched merchant relationships. The account qualifies the explanation for their longer journeys: a formal prohibition is not established; existing Rudok business relationships may have constrained entry. Preserve this distinction between documented exclusion and inferred commercial barriers.
- **Printed pp. 99–100:** Shamma sold pashm for cash, purchased salt in bulk, then exchanged it for grain or apricots for further sale. Rizvi describes profits over repeated transactions. The argument directly complicates “barter therefore no calculation” and “subsistence therefore no profit”.
- **Printed p. 100:** household labour sustained lengthy trading absences; the joint family allowed other members to continue agricultural work. This offers a concrete link between commercial activity and social organisation, without assuming that all trade rested on ceremonial friendship.
- **Printed pp. 100–101:** Zanskar was supplied through Rupshu while other salt circuits centred on Chemrey. The Lo-pchak combined ceremonial offerings, political privilege and profitable trading. Religious form and commercial benefit were compatible.
- **Printed pp. 102–103:** official missions had privileged transport and political protection, whereas private caravans faced costs, weather and robbery. The rise of the Kalimpong route altered the viability of Leh–Lhasa traffic before the final closing of western Tibetan trade. This further resists a uniform chronology of “the salt route”.
- **Printed p. 103:** the much-celebrated Central Asian luxury trade benefited Ladakh through transport and services, but the traded products were mostly neither produced nor consumed there. Economic significance must be measured for particular groups and activities; transit value alone does not demonstrate local dependence.

**A further original article now checked:** Rizvi (1999b), ‘A self-reliant economy’, *Ladakh Studies* 12, pp. 31–34, explicitly means financing imports without external subsidies, not producing everything locally. Her historical reconstruction combines documents and retired traders' recollections (pp. 31–32); p. 34 argues that provisioning circuits mattered more locally than prestigious long-distance trade. Its brief summary of *Trans-Himalayan Caravans* is valuable but does not substitute for that monograph. The salt–barley rate at Chemrey is around 1:1 here (p. 33), compared with around 2:1 salt to barley in *Ladakh* (1996, p. 100): retain place, date, commodity, units and source rather than asserting one customary rate. Her explanation of lost self-reliance gives external political rupture particular weight (p. 34); use it alongside the other authors' attention to longer-term infrastructure and household changes. [Original issue](https://ladakhstudies.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/03/ls12.pdf).

**Pashm comparison and an author's correction:** Rizvi's 1999 pashm chapter opens by locating trade within Ladakh's political history (p. 317). Its online version includes a separately dated 2023 addendum, which corrects the original explanation of the shawl industry's decline: the 1870 Franco-Prussian War should not be used as a sufficient explanation (unpaginated PDF p. 14). The addendum also notes later changes in marketing organisations and fibre processing (PDF p. 15). This is important evidence that interpretations and production chains have histories of their own; the original 1999 chapter must not be cited for a correction made in 2023. [Original and addendum](https://ladakhstudies.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/rrol8_24_rizvi-update_ocred_compressed.pdf).

The earlier 1995 journal article distinguishes local provisioning trade from long-distance commerce at its opening and presents mountain passes as communication routes rather than absolute barriers. It is useful orientation but is less suitable than the directly inspected book pages for the institutional argument. [Original article](https://www.himalayanclub.org/hj/51/13/merchants-and-mountains/).

## Alam: salt politics and the making of economic space

### Mandi: connect fiscal change with political legitimacy

Alam's *Becoming India* explains Mandi salt within the development of British paramountcy and local political authority. Printed pp. 173–174 establish the Raja's position in a polity whose legal ruler was Madho Rao and describe the gradual intrusion of British officers. This context is necessary to understand why a salt measure could also be a dispute about legitimate rule.

On **p. 175**, untaxed Mandi salt circulating into British territory prompted the 1870 intervention. Mandi's response invoked an existing tribute settlement and the Raja's prestige. British revenue claims overruled that objection. On **pp. 175–176**, the new administration barred salt-for-grain/ghee, stopped treasury/Zakat-Khana orders and purchaser labour, and introduced a paid workforce. On **pp. 176–177**, unrest during Shivratri is connected both to the new terms of access to salt and to affronts to royal dignity. Alam explicitly notes that his sources are British official records. Complaints about cattle and up-front cash payments are therefore historical grievances mediated through those records, not independently measured nutritional outcomes.

On **p. 178**, Alam reads the changing official labels for the episode—progressively diminishing its seriousness—as evidence of the reputational interests of both British officer and Raja. The 1874 restoration of administration to the Raja did not, in his account, restore the old payment arrangements. His interpretation distinguishes royal reconciliation from continued peasant grievance. That distinction is much more useful than claiming that “the community” resisted or won as a unit.

**An important qualification from the original gazetteer.** Emerson's salt section records a continuing mixture of cash payment and labour. Printed **p. 163** (PDF 176) says traders paid a carriage charge while agriculturalists and others taking headloads were exempt from it but owed a day of unpaid work. Printed **p. 164** (PDF 177) repeats headload work as a current condition. Printed **p. 160** (PDF 173) also describes the replacement of an earlier labour system by paid labour. These passages may distinguish purchasing obligations from the mine's regular labour force; they might also record changes after 1871. The inspected evidence cannot decide between those possibilities. The literature review should present the tension and avoid claiming a universal, irreversible move from labour/in-kind arrangements to cash. The detailed resolution belongs in Chapter 2 and archival work.

Further useful gazetteer checks are **p. 159** (PDF 172), where demand persists despite cheaper Khewra salt and the author reports local beliefs about bodily/livestock effects; **p. 161** (PDF 174), where customary privileges, sales bonuses and propitiation of quarry goddesses accompany the organised workforce; and **p. 167** (PDF 180), where salt is exported alongside other products and transport has several animal and human forms. These are attributed administrative descriptions. Do not present reported medicinal beliefs as verified health effects or the gazetteer's favourable judgement of wages as a neutral measure of miners' welfare.

### Commodity circuits: markets help constitute borders

Alam (2021) offers the clearest already-existing regional theory of circuits. It compares Lavi and the growing Shimla market through travel accounts, administrative records, trade figures and regional historical scholarship. **Printed p. 173** argues that trade and borders should be treated as mutually constitutive; **p. 174** extends Anderson's analysis of meaningful journeys to commodity circulation. The result is an interpretation to engage, not merely a source of names for a graph.

The mechanism is concrete. **Printed pp. 176–178** place Lavi among several competing and complementary markets and connect it with Gartok. **Printed pp. 179–181** distinguish high-volume transit commerce from the much less monetised economies of many neighbouring peasant communities. **Printed pp. 182–185** explain how the Shimla market drew local labour, cultivation and products into new cash relationships and diverted some previously trans-Himalayan commerce. **Printed pp. 188–189** argue that this reorientation helped make political boundaries appear natural. Alam expressly does not describe British arrival as a deliberate blanket destruction of trade: commercial encouragement and the later overshadowing of older circuits coexist in his account (p. 188, checked visually).

**How to compare critically.** Alam's low-monetisation argument concerns particular hill-state peasant economies and the contrast between Shimla and Lavi. It should not cancel Rizvi's account of cash and commercial calculation among Ladakhi traders; the populations, periodisation and economic scales differ. Likewise, the chapter's political-economic explanation cannot establish by itself that a particular Tso Kar institution was transformed by colonial taxation. Its challenge to Scott's mountain refuge model on pp. 175 and 185–187 concerns *The Art of Not Being Governed* (2009); that is distinct from Scott's 1976 moral-economy argument used elsewhere in Chapter 1.

## Dollfus and Singh et al.: change without a single Himalayan chronology

**Dollfus's evidence and method.** The article's main case is Kharnak, based on about twenty years of fieldwork, set alongside historical descriptions (**p. 61**). It should not be labelled a universal Rupshu ethnography. It questions the assumption that present pastoralists must descend from a continuous ancient nomadic population; for Kharnak, agro-pastoral transhumance followed by greater mobility is a plausible alternative (**pp. 61–62**). This is a direct corrective to evolutionary or timeless portraits of Himalayan communities.

**Ecological organisation and inequality.** On **pp. 64–65**, altitude, the short growing season and differentiated uses of livestock help explain pastoral practice. Movements are planned within defended territories, not aimless wandering. Territories themselves change with demography and politics. Wealthier households own more animals; poorer ones also sell their labour. Ecological adaptation therefore did not mean absence of internal economic differentiation.

**Later political economy.** On **pp. 66–67**, border conflict, displaced people, the loss of pasture and the military presence alter routes, territories and economic options. Her salt-and-pasture argument explicitly draws on Ahmed, so this part is not independent corroboration of Ahmed's pre-1960 reconstruction. On **pp. 67–68**, roads and urban traders bring new forms of market access. Pashmina's rise changes animal composition and even the valuation of goats in religious and social giving. This is a particularly effective link between changing exchange values and changing cultural values. On **pp. 68–70**, state support, provisioning, tourism, education and household labour contribute to livelihood changes; **pp. 70–71** show how migration and generational aspirations differ. The article does not support attributing every change to British colonial government.

**Singh et al.'s different evidential contribution.** The 2013 study combines 80 semi-structured interviews during 2004–2008, local departmental records, recollections of six elderly people, other community testimony, spatial mapping and wildlife observations (**pp. 2–3**). Historical pasture polygons are expressly approximate, and past social history is reconstructed retrospectively. Contemporary ecological observations are not the same kind of evidence as retrospective dates.

The authors link the post-1962 restriction of winter pastures with changed pastoral organisation, Tibetan refugee settlement, government pashmina policy and subsidised supplies (**pp. 6–8**). Their Tso Kar case distinguishes intensified use of remaining land from the older seasonal pattern (**pp. 9–10**). Roads, army employment, public services and urban opportunities also alter labour and household cooperation (**pp. 10–11, 14**). Their policy proposals balance income and biodiversity and sometimes favour fewer pastoralists (**pp. 13–14**); that is a normative policy conclusion to assess, not a neutral description of inevitable decline.

**Two cautions when comparing these studies.** First, Singh et al. use a relatively categorical statement that market exchange replaced barter (**p. 10**), whereas Ahmed documents barter alongside occasional sale in 1992 (**pp. 45–46**). The observations concern different dates and scopes; distinguish increasing market dependence from the demonstrated disappearance of every barter practice. Secondly, Singh et al.'s retrospective pre-1962 Tso Kar collection statement (**p. 9**) is more confident than Ahmed's historical assessment (**p. 40**). The thesis should preserve that discrepancy. Repeating a later review's settled phrasing does not resolve the earlier evidential problem.

## Proposed additions to the chapter

1. **A paragraph comparing provisioning with profit.** Lead with Rizvi, pp. 97–100, then use Ahmed, pp. 45–47, to show that changed returns and persistent meanings are related but different questions. Add Nepal comparisons from the parallel evidence memo rather than calling them Ladakhi institutions.
2. **A paragraph on labour, membership and gender.** Use Ahmed, pp. 41–43 and p. 45 notes 31–32; connect with the household organisation in Rizvi p. 100. This gives the sociology material institutions rather than only trust.
3. **A paragraph on property, monasteries and disputed authority.** Use Ahmed, pp. 43–45. Explain the different roles of Thugje, Korzok and Hemis. Avoid a generic redistributive monastery.
4. **A paragraph reviewing salt politics through Mandi.** Present Alam, pp. 175–178, as a geographically specific historical interpretation. A short footnote can flag the gazetteer's continuing headload obligation; detailed reconciliation belongs in Chapter 2.
5. **A paragraph on changing circuits and border formation.** Engage Alam 2021, pp. 173–174, 184–189, and distinguish this interpretative achievement from the proposed computational contribution.
6. **A paragraph comparing post-1962 reorganisation.** Pair Dollfus's Kharnak case with Singh et al.'s Rupshu work; include the discrepancy on early Tso Kar extraction and the different research methods. Neither should be flattened into evidence for a colonial rupture.
7. **A bounded gap statement.** Explain that the thesis compares source-attributed commodity, community, place and authority relations, while preserving disagreement and asking whether salt occupied a connective position in the selected corpus. “Backbone” remains an empirical proposition, not the literature review's conclusion.
