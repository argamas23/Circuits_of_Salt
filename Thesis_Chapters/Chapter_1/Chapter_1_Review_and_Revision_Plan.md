**1. Replace anonymous consensus with named scholarly engagement**

On PDF pp. 8–10, phrases such as “historical studies ... have repeatedly demonstrated” and “anthropological research shows” stand in for discussion of actual works. The reader cannot assess whose evidence concerns Ladakh, whose concerns Nepal, which period is covered, or whether the authors offer different explanations.

For each important group of studies, establish four things in connected prose: its research setting and evidence; its principal argument; its contribution to the question of salt; and the limit or question left for this thesis. Compare works around a historical problem rather than producing a separate mini-biography of each author.

For example, place Rizvi's account of overlapping subsistence and commercial circuits beside Ross's account of trading adaptation. Then explain how both complicate the assumption that ecological dependence excluded profit. This constitutes a literature review because it assesses interpretations, rather than simply listing goods and routes.

**2. Correct the separation between reciprocity and commercial calculation**

The draft repeatedly opposes socially embedded exchange to commercial calculation. On PDF p. 9, trading partnerships are presented as extending beyond commercial calculation without acknowledging how they might also support it. The conclusion describes a shift from reciprocal exchange to regulated markets. These formulations risk making reciprocity, profit, money, and political regulation appear mutually exclusive.

Rizvi provides a direct corrective in a book already in `Books/`: *Ladakh: Crossroads of High Asia*, second edition, 1996. Printed pp. 99–100 (PDF pp. 121–122) describe Shamma traders selling pashm for cash, bargaining, buying salt in bulk, and making profits through repeated exchanges. Printed pp. 97–98 distinguish the salt–grain–wool subsistence circuit from the high-value pashm trade while showing their connections.

Ross's Humla article is equally useful. Printed p. 43 identifies profit from differing salt–grain exchange ratios; p. 44 discusses variable prices alongside ritualised trading friendships; p. 48 examines adaptation through the incorporation of Indian salt. That is a comparative Nepalese case, not direct evidence about Rupshu. Lecoq's printed pp. 47–48 likewise bring ceremonial friendship together with negotiated barter and advantageous exchange locations.

The stronger interpretation is that social obligations organised and constrained economic calculation in particular settings. Whether they protected subsistence, distributed benefits unequally, supported profit, or did several things together is a question for the sources.

**3. Restore uncertainty and chronology to the Tso Kar discussion**

PDF p. 9 presents Tso Kar's customary extraction arrangements without a date; p. 10 links the regional discussion to a social order existing before colonial intervention. This arrangement encourages the reader to treat practices documented much later as a stable precolonial system.

Ahmed's chapter is more historically complicated. Its opening distinguishes the importance of Tibetan salt supplies from Tso Kar's greater prominence after border closure. Printed pp. 38–40 compare oral accounts with earlier written descriptions and explicitly leave uncertainty about extraction and trade before the 1960s. The nineteenth-century evidence should not be discarded, but neither should the conflicting accounts be flattened into a single continuous history.

Printed p. 42 states that the described labour-group system began when Rupshu started removing salt from Tso Kar. It also says that men in a *chu lag* are not necessarily members of the same *pha spun*. The latter corrects a claim in earlier project writing that the work teams simply corresponded to patri-fraternal groups; Chapter 1 should not inherit that claim during expansion. The page was checked against the scan image as well as extracted text.

Dollfus, printed pp. 66–68, and Singh et al., PDF pp. 6 and 10, place major changes in mobility, supplies, pastoral production, and markets in the post-1962 setting. Review their interpretations explicitly. Do not turn evidence about postcolonial roads, military presence, rations, or pashmina policy into evidence for a British colonial mechanism.

**4. Make the sociology of salt more than a statement about trust**

The existing sociology concentrates on reciprocity, kinship, and cohesion. The available sources also allow discussion of who worked, who was entitled to extract, how shares were allocated, who was excluded, and how resource claims were defended.

Ahmed's printed pp. 41–42 discuss predominantly male salt work and organised allocation. Printed pp. 43–45 describe conflict, administrative involvement, courts, the Ladakh Buddhist Association, access rules, and claims involving pasture as well as salt. Printed p. 45 distinguishes household retention, religious contributions, barter, and sale; p. 47 discusses male identity. These are different social relationships, not interchangeable examples of reciprocity.

The monastery paragraph on Chapter 1 PDF p. 9 particularly needs qualification. Contributions to Korzok and Thugje do not by themselves prove a comprehensive redistributive or protective system. Ahmed also discusses Hemis backing Kharnak's claims. Religious institutions can therefore appear in conflict and unequal access as well as in redistribution. Review the specific role documented for each institution.

**5. Strengthen the politics through historical scholarship**

The political argument currently relies heavily on a general model of colonial regulation. Two works by Alam already available locally can give it a much more specific historiographical basis.

In *Becoming India*, printed pp. 175–178 (PDF pp. 199–202), Alam analyses the intervention in Mandi salt administration, altered payment and labour arrangements, the opposition those measures generated, and the settlement that followed. His account relates economic change to both popular grievances and royal authority. This is a precise point of comparison with moral-economy questions; it should not be treated as a direct account of Tso Kar. The underlying Foreign Department records cited by Alam remain distinct from his interpretation and have not been checked here.

In “Commodity Journeys and Market Circuits: Making Borders ‘Natural’ in Colonial Western Himalayas” (2021), printed pp. 173–174 (PDF pp. 5–6), Alam explicitly links commodity circulation to border formation. The chapter compares the Lavi fair and Shimla market. This means the thesis cannot describe the existing field as merely descriptive or claim that the politics of circuits has been ignored. It needs to position its salt-specific, corpus-based inquiry in relation to that argument. The relevant p. 173 was also inspected as a scan image. [Publisher record](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/south-asian-borderlands/commodity-journeys-and-market-circuits-making-borders-natural-in-colonial-western-himalayas/D8031ACE2E771505CBDAE60E1D3A23CC).

Jagjeet Lally's “Salt and Sovereignty in Colonial Burma” (2021) is also already in `References/`. It examines salt as an instrument of political authority while emphasising negotiation, uneven state capacity, and the limits of restriction. One compact comparative discussion would connect the Himalayan review with wider political histories of salt without importing Burma's history into Ladakh. The local PDF is an accepted manuscript; its PDF page numbers must not be converted into final journal page references without checking. [Author's institutional record](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10108358/).

**6. Establish a narrower research gap**

On PDF pp. 10–11, the discussion moves from geographically specific studies and dispersed archival evidence to an asserted inability to view the salt economy as an integrated system. This needs revision for two reasons. First, local focus does not make a study merely descriptive. Second, dispersed evidence is an archival problem; it does not by itself demonstrate a historiographical omission.

Rizvi already reconstructs connected circuits, Ahmed joins extraction to social institutions and contested rights, and Alam connects circulation to border-making. Explain what a defined computational comparison can add to those achievements.

A defensible formulation is: the thesis will bring source-attributed relations concerning selected commodities, communities, places, and authorities into a common representation, then assess salt's connective position within that corpus alongside close reading. Whether this is a distinctive contribution relative to the wider field remains a question to establish through the literature, not an exhaustive novelty claim already proved.

The sentence on PDF p. 8 asserting that salt routes “functioned as the infrastructural backbone” should become a research question. Chapter 1 should establish why that proposition is worth testing; Chapter 4 should evaluate it.

**Model paragraph: the proposed approach, not a replacement draft**

Rizvi's account of Ladakhi trade complicates a strict opposition between subsistence exchange and commercial activity. She distinguishes the salt–grain–wool trade from the high-value pashm circuit, but shows how Shamma traders participated in both. Their transactions combined barter, cash sales, and profit through repeated exchange (Rizvi, 1996, pp. 97–100). Ahmed, working at the scale of the Rupshu household and salt trek, draws attention to a different set of relationships: organised labour, allocation of extraction areas, household consumption, religious contributions, and exchange (Ahmed, 1999, pp. 41–45). Her discussion of competing accounts of Tso Kar's earlier exploitation also cautions against treating the arrangements observed during her fieldwork as an unchanged inheritance from the precolonial period (pp. 38–40). Read together, these studies suggest that salt circulation connected provisioning, commercial calculation, and social authority, while leaving the form and chronology of those connections open to investigation. The question is therefore how particular institutions shaped production and exchange, and how their roles changed, rather than whether Himalayan trade belonged exclusively to a reciprocal or a market economy.
